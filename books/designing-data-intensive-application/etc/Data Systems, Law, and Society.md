**Data Systems, Law, and Society**
- Data systems are not shaped only by technical goals; they also affect the people and institutions that rely on them.
- The chapter argues that engineers have a responsibility to society, not just to the business that pays for the system.
- Privacy law such as the GDPR gives people stronger control over personal data, including consent, access, and deletion rights.
- Consent must be informed, specific, and freely given; dark patterns or pre-ticked boxes do not count as meaningful consent.
- Data residency and localization rules can require data to stay in a particular jurisdiction, so region-aware sharding can be useful.
- If a system is designed so each person or tenant has isolated storage, export and deletion become much easier to implement.
- The book warns that behavioral data is often treated as “data exhaust,” but it is really a source of power and prediction.
- Once a service collects user behavior at scale, it can drift from serving the user to serving advertisers or other third parties.
- This is why the book frames many internet services as surveillance systems, even when that word feels uncomfortable.
- The social impact is not abstract: automated systems can influence news consumption, politics, loans, insurance, and hiring decisions.
- The chapter’s core message is to minimize collection, avoid retaining data forever, and reduce the incentives for ubiquitous surveillance.
- Technologists should think about societal consequences while designing systems, not after the damage is already done.

**TL;DR**
- Build systems that respect privacy, limit collection, support deletion, and treat legal and social consequences as part of the design.
